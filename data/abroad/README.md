# Abroad inscriptions and names: the two lists

**List 1, `abroad_inscriptions.csv`** (built by `scripts/build_abroad_list.py`): every Indus inscription found outside the Indus region in the project's sources (export, held-out-from-export set, Laursen 2010 Fig. 11, extra_texts). 42 texts after removing duplicates. Columns mark where a "man" sign (G90 and its compounds, G91 twin men) sits and which signs follow it.

Descriptive comparison (abroad texts already seen, so not a test):

| | Home (exploration, 1,523 texts) | Abroad (42) |
|---|---|---|
| Contain a "man" sign | 8.6% | 52% |
| Start with a "man" sign | 1.9% | 33% |

- `G415 G803 G1` opens two Gulf seals (Ur; "Dilmun"). `G415 G803` never occurs together in the home texts.
- G91 (twin men) is the whole text on the Kalba seal and on Laursen seal 6 (Qal'at al-Bahrain).
- G55 (twelve strokes) occurs on 5 Gulf seals.

**List 2, `cuneiform_names.csv`**: personal names of Meluhhan and Dilmun people attested in cuneiform. It is short: only two names (Nanaza, Samar) are plausibly in the Meluhhan language and of the right date (c. 2030 BC); the Dilmun royal names (Ri'mum, Yagli-El, Sumu-lel) are Amorite and some 300 years later than the Gulf Type seals.

**What can and cannot be done with them.** Two names are too few for any test. What the lists support: the Gulf texts after a "man" sign are short (mostly 1–3 signs), the length expected for a short name written in syllables or a name of one or two word-signs. Nanaza has a repeated first syllable (na-na); if Gulf scribes spelled such names by sound, a doubled sign would be expected sometimes, but no abroad text has a sign repeated next to itself after a man sign. A stronger list needs the Dilmun-trade texts from Ur (Old Babylonian) and the Ur III texts naming Dilmun and Meluhha people, which list travellers and traders by name.

## Seal-legend formula: "NAME, son of NAME" (added 26 October 2026)

A round seal with an Indus bull in Paris carries a cuneiform legend read (preliminarily, Glassner 2002) as **Ur-Ninildum dumu Ur-gi**: "Ur-Ninildum, son of Ur-gi". Mesopotamian owner legends follow this pattern. Vidale (2004) suggests the Indus "man" sign may have marked descent ("son of") on western seals.

Where the man sign sits in the 42 abroad texts (descriptive): alone 3, first 11, **between two sign groups 7**, last 1. The seven: Bahrain `G160 | G90 | G55 G60 G190`; Saar `G55 G220 | G91 | G1 G93 G31`; Janabiyah `… G829 G55 | G125 | G90 G55`; Susa `G365 | G90 | G407 G604 G700`; Ur `G350 | G90 | G4`; Gonur `G285 G2 G235 G220 | G125 | G590 G390 G740`; unprovenanced `? | G91 | G603 G604`.

So abroad texts show both shapes a name formula would produce: "MAN + name" (like the cuneiform personal determinative) and "name + MAN + name" (like "X son of Y"). G55 (twelve strokes) appears right next to the man sign on three Bahrain-area seals.

## Names or invocations? (Laursen 2016, added 26 October 2026)

Laursen (2016, *Arabian Archaeology and Epigraphy* 27: 2–18) notes the "abnormally high frequency of a 'twins sign'" on early Gulf Type seals and reads it as a sign of messages composed in a local concept or language. Later Dilmun seals (style III, c. 1600 BC) carry Akkadian **invocations of the god Inzak**, not owners' names, and Dilmun's later symbol system belonged to the royal house and the Inzak cult. So the man/twin-men signs on the early Gulf seals could mark an invocation or an institution rather than a person.

How to tell the two apart: an invocation or title formula should **repeat exactly** across seals; personal names should **vary**. In the 42 abroad texts, G91 alone repeats (Kalba; Laursen 6, Qal'at al-Bahrain) and `G415 G803 G1` repeats (Ur; "Dilmun"), which leans towards formulas or titles for at least part of the material.

Possible further abroad material named by Laursen (2016): post-firing graffiti on an Indus sherd from the A'ali royal mound, Tomb 28 (Mackay 1929, pl. VI); marked Indus sherds from Qal'at al-Bahrain, c. 2100 BC (Højlund 1994b, figs 337, 374, 378). Check against ICIT before use.

## The Gulf seals probably write a Semitic language (Laursen 2023; added 26 October 2026)

Laursen (2023, "The City of Dilmun and Evolving Technology, c. 2000 BC") states that the Indus-sign sequences on the circular Gulf Type seals "differ from those attested from the Indus world, suggesting that this newly introduced writing technology … was adapted for use with a different, most probably Semitic, language" (citing Parpola 1994; Vidale 2004, 2005; Laursen 2010). These seals always show a bull, and were locally made in Dilmun "in limited numbers" (Laursen 2010; Uesugi forthcoming).

**Why it matters.** If the Gulf seals write a Semitic language, the expected content is known in shape: Semitic personal names of the period are mostly two elements, a divine name plus a verb or noun (e.g. *Yagli-El* "the god has shown himself", *Sumu-lel*), and the divine element repeats across many names. Prediction for future Gulf finds: a small set of signs should recur at one edge of the name part (the god), with varied signs next to them.

Leads named in Laursen 2023 for new material:
- Uesugi (forthcoming) on the locally made Gulf Type seals with Indus script from Bahrain.
- Laursen, Raun & Herslund (in preparation), new excavations at Qala'at al-Bahrain 2020–2021.
- Laursen, Al Shaikh, Lentz, Lombard & Marchesi (in preparation), tokens with cuneiform-inscribed seal impressions from Bahrain.

Observation to check later (speculative): G55, twelve strokes, recurs next to the man sign on Bahrain-area seals. In Mesopotamia several gods were written with numbers (e.g. Sin = 30, Shamash = 20, Adad = 10). A number sign standing for a god inside names would fit the two-element pattern, but 12 is not a standard divine number, and this is a pattern from 3 seals.

Update (26 October 2026): G55 is now confirmed as a number-word at home, not an ordinary count (`results/g55_closer_2026-10-26.md`, p 0.033), and it is the only numeral that behaves this way clearly (`results/numeral_word_scouting.md`). The idea that G55 is a divine number is still weak. In the standard Mesopotamian system (Anu 60, Enlil 50, Ea 40, Sin 30, Shamash 20, Ishtar 15, Nergal 14, Adad 10 or 6), no major god is 12. The system is also attested mainly from the second and first millennia BC, later than the Gulf Type seals. This was checked from secondary sources only; it needs an Assyriologist's confirmation.
