# Indus ↔ cuneiform contact points: candidate outside anchors

Compiled 2026-10-22 for the Indus-Research- project (Erin Rae Toppe).

**Grades:** A = solid, published primary evidence that I checked against a museum record or a scholarly edition. B = plausible, or published but checked only through a reliable secondary source. C = speculative.

**Rule followed:** I give no reading that I could not trace to a named scholar. "UNVERIFIED" marks every detail I could not check directly.

**Bottom line up front:** No true bilingual exists, meaning there is no object that carries the same text in both Indus signs and cuneiform. The two scripts never appear together on one surface in any find I could locate.

- Every anchor below is indirect. Each is one of the following:
  - an Indus-format object that carries cuneiform but no Indus signs;
  - a cuneiform text naming Meluhhans, their goods or their language;
  - an Indus inscription found abroad in a datable or culturally specific context.
- The most testable lines are:
  - personal-name hypotheses, linking Gulf/Ur Indus inscriptions to Meluhhan names in cuneiform;
  - commodity and animal lexemes;
  - metrology, where the Dilmun weight standard equals the Indus standard.

**Access limits during this search:**
- cdli.earth / cdli.mpiwg-berlin.mpg.de was blocked by proxy/permission. That blocked the P-number checks: P432309 (Ibbi-Sin), P461937 (Sargon), the Nisaba 15 texts and the CDLI seal records.

## PART 1. Objects combining Indus features with cuneiform

### 1.1 Indus-style square seal with a cuneiform legend: "Gadd seal no. 1", Ur
- **What it is:** A rectangular stamp seal (27 × 24 mm) of green-grey mottled steatite with a pierced lug.
  - It shows a crude bull standing left. One line of cuneiform runs above the bull.
  - It has no Indus signs. Indus format and iconography carry a cuneiform legend in the place where an Indus seal carries its inscription.
- **Where it is now:** British Museum ME 120573, reg. 1928,1009.56, excavation no. U.7683.
  - It was a surface find at Diqdiqqah (Ur), excavated by Woolley in 1926/27.
  - It is on display in Gallery 33. The BM dates it to "c. 2500 BC".
- **What the cuneiform says:** The BM record gives no transcription. Vidale (2004: 264) calls the inscription "hard to read and controversial".
  - Parpola (1994: 131), as quoted second-hand, gives the signs as (1) sag(k) or ka, (2) ku, lu or ma, (3) zi or ba, with possibly a fourth sign.
  - The "commonest value" quoted is sag-ku-zi. I could not confirm whether that sentence is Parpola's.
  - Gadd's own reading: UNVERIFIED.
  - Other discussions: Parpola, Parpola & Brunswig 1977 and Peyronel 2000 (per Vidale).
- **Bibliography:**
  - Gadd 1932, "Seals of ancient Indian style found at Ur", *Proc. British Academy* 18: 191–210 (no. 1, pp. 5–6, pl. I:1).
  - Mitchell 1986, "Indus and Gulf type seals from Ur", in Al-Khalifa & Rice (eds.), *Bahrain through the Ages*, no. 7, p. 280, fig. 111.
  - Aruz & Wallenfels 2003, *Art of the First Cities*, no. 301b, p. 410.
  - Woolley 1956, *UE IV*, p. 174.
- **URL:** https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/W_1928-1009-56
- **Usefulness for anchoring:** Low to moderate.
  - If the cuneiform is the owner's name, it shows that Indus-format seals could carry a personal name in the inscription slot. That supports the hypothesis that Indus seal texts are names or titles.
  - It cannot pin any Indus sign sequence, because there is no Indus text on it.
  - It does pin the bull-without-manger iconography to a name-bearing legend.
- **Grade:** A for the object. C for any reading of the legend.

### 1.2 Indus bull seal with Sumerian name, Cabinet des Médailles (BnF), Paris
- **What it is:** A seal with an "Indus bull with a lowered head" and a cuneiform inscription.
  - It has no Indus signs.
  - Vidale (2004: 264–265) calls it a cylinder seal. Its form is otherwise UNVERIFIED.
  - Vidale (2004: 270) says it was bought on the Beirut antiquities market, so it is unprovenanced.
  - It is unpublished. Publication by D. Arnaud was announced, per Glassner.
- **Where it is now:** Cabinet des Médailles, BnF, Paris. Inventory number UNVERIFIED and not found.
- **What the cuneiform says:** J.-J. Glassner (2002) gives a provisional reading: **Ur-{d}Nin-ildum dumu Ur-gi7**, "Ur-Ninildum, son of Ur-gi ('Dog')".
  - Source: Vidale 2004: 265.
  - Glassner 2002: "Dilmun et Magan…", in Cleuziou, Tosi & Zarins (eds.), *Essays on the Late Prehistory of the Arabian Peninsula*, Rome, 337–381.
- **URL (Vidale PDF):** https://www.harappa.com/sites/default/files/201402/Vidale-Indus-Mesopotamia.pdf
- **Usefulness:** Low. It shows Indus-derived iconography used by a person with a fully Sumerian name and patronymic, which fits the pattern of Meluhhan acculturation.
  - It gives no Indus text to pin.
  - Authenticity and provenance are unknown.
- **Grade:** C. Unpublished, unprovenanced, and the reading is provisional.

### 1.3 Cylinder seal of Šu-ilišu, "interpreter of Meluhha"
- **What it is:** A Late Akkadian (or early Ur III) cylinder seal with a presentation scene. Porada noted that it had been re-cut.
  - Secondary descriptions mention a small figure carrying an animal (goat or antelope) on the arm of a figure before a seated deity. The exact iconography is UNVERIFIED by me.
  - It has no Indus signs.
- **Where it is now:** Musée du Louvre, Dept. of Near Eastern Antiquities. It came from the de Clercq collection (de Clercq 1888).
  - The inventory number **AO 22310** is given by Wikipedia/handwiki captions. I could not confirm it on collections.louvre.fr.
- **What the cuneiform says:** Šu-ilišu / EME.BAL / me-luḫ-ḫa{ki}, "Šu-ilišu, interpreter of (the language of) Meluhha".
  - Vermaak (2008: 455 n. 5) cites "ema-bal me-luḫ-ḫa" (sic).
  - Steve Tinney confirmed the reading for Possehl (Possehl, *Expedition* 48/1, 2006).
- **Bibliography:**
  - Edzard 1968–69, "Die Inschriften der altakkadischen Rollsiegel", *AfO* 22, no. 33 (cited second-hand).
  - Possehl 2006, "Shu-ilishu's cylinder seal", *Expedition* 48(1): 42–43. URL: https://www.penn.museum/sites/expedition/shu-ilishus-cylinder-seal/
  - Sollberger 1970, *Bull. Univ. London* 8–9: 247–250.
  - Aruz & Wallenfels 2003 (*Art of the First Cities* exhibition).
- **Usefulness:** It is the strongest evidence that Meluhhan was a distinct spoken language needing professional translators in Akkadian Babylonia (c. 2200–2100 BC).
  - It justifies treating Indus inscriptions as recording a non-Sumerian, non-Akkadian language.
  - It pins no sign sequence.
- **Grade:** A for the text and its meaning. B for the Louvre number.

### 1.4 Akkadian cylinder seal of Ibni-šarrum (water-buffalo iconography plus cuneiform)
- **What it is:** A seal of the scribe Ibni-šarrum naming Šar-kali-šarri, c. 2217–2193 BC. It shows water buffaloes, an animal thought to be imported from the Indus.
- **Where it is now:** Louvre AO 22303 (per Wikipedia; not checked on the Louvre site).
- **Usefulness:** Very low. It is an iconographic borrowing only. Its one use is to show that Indus fauna had prestige value in Akkadian court art.
- **Grade:** B.

### 1.5 Gulf-type seal with a Linear Elamite inscription (Laursen 2010 seal no. 28)
- **What it is:** A round "Gulf type" seal from the western Iranian plateau. It carries a Linear Elamite inscription instead of Indus signs.
  - Winkelmann (1999: 28 n. 14) reads it as a proper noun, *tash-shi-te* or *tash-shi-hu* (cited by Laursen 2010: 118).
  - It is dated by Puzur-Inšušinak to c. 2112–2095 BC (Laursen 2010: 120).
- **Where it is now:** UNVERIFIED.
- **Usefulness:** Moderate as an indirect anchor. The Gulf-seal format, the same seal type that elsewhere carries Indus signs, here carries a personal name in a script that is now largely deciphered (Desset et al. 2022).
  - This strengthens the inference that the inscriptions on Indus-inscribed Gulf seals are personal names. It is a structural anchor: it predicts the "slot" content.
- **Grade:** B.

## PART 2. Meluhhans, their language, names, places and goods in cuneiform

### 2.1 Meluhhan personal names (the only plausible native names)
**Na-na-za and Sa?-ma-ar (Samar)**
- **The text:** Ur III, from Urusagrig, dated Šu-Suen year 6. Edition: **Nisaba 15 371: 1–7**.
- **The entries:**
  - 1 sila3 of sesame oil (i3-giš) to Na-na-za.
  - 1 sila3 to Samar. The blog renders this "Sab-ma-ar"; the exact first sign is UNVERIFIED.
  - ½ sila3 to A-li-a-ḫi, his wife, who has an Akkadian name.
- **The heading:** i3-ba lu2 Me-luḫ-ḫa{ki}-me a-ru-a lugal sipad adara4-me. This translates as "oil ration of the Meluhhans; royal dedicated (slaves), shepherds of bezoar goats".
- **Scholarly view:** Laursen & Steinkeller (2017: 83–84), *Babylonia, the Gulf Region and the Indus* (Mesopotamian Civilizations 21, Eisenbrauns), call these "the only evidence available" bearing on the Meluhhan language.
- **URL (summary):** https://www.harappa.com/blog/only-known-meluhan-personal-names-samar-and-nanaza
- **Usefulness: HIGH (relative to everything else).** These are two phonetic name shapes, CV-CV-CV and CV-CVC, that probably belong to Meluhhans, or at least to people classed as Meluhhan.
  - Indus seal inscriptions are widely taken to contain names or titles. These names give a phonotactic test bed: a phonetic hypothesis for the Indus syllabary should be able to produce such shapes.
  - They do not pin specific signs.
- **Grade:** A for the text as published by Steinkeller. B for the claim that the names are Indus-language names.

**Lu2-sun2-zi-da ("Lu-sunzida"), lu2 me-luḫ-ḫa, "man of Meluhha"**
- **The text:** Sargonic (Akkadian-period) Lagash. Lu-sunzida pays 10 shekels of silver to Urur, son of Amar-lu(ku), as compensation for a broken tooth.
  - The name is Sumerian: "man of the just/true wild cow (sun2)".
  - Parpola, Parpola & Brunswig (1977) and Laursen (2010: 118) suggest it calques a Meluhhan name with a bovine element. Vidale (2004: 263, 268–269) suggests the gaur.
- **Text edition / museum number:** UNVERIFIED. It is not found in the sources I could open; it is cited via Parpola et al. 1977 (JESHO 20: 129–165) and Vidale 2004: 263.
- **Usefulness:** Moderate for a hypothesis test. If Indus seal texts contain theophoric or bovine names, a sequence like "man/person sign + bovine-related sign" could be a structural parallel.
  - Laursen (2010: 116–118) notes that the "man" and "twins" signs prefix Gulf-seal inscriptions, possibly as name markers.
- **Grade:** B for the attestation. C for the calque theory.

**Ur-{d}Lamma dumu me-luḫ-ḫa, "Ur-Lamma, son of a Meluhhan" / "the Meluhhan"**
- **Where attested:** In Ur III Girsu, he seals (kišib) documents.
  - OBTR 242 (Šulgi 40)
  - CT 3 17 = BM 14594 (Amar-Suen 1)
  - UDT 64 (NBC 64)
  - Source: Vermaak 2008: 466, 469; Parpola et al. 1977, texts 02 and 07.
- **Usefulness:** Low for language. The name is fully Sumerian, an acculturated second generation. It does show that Meluhhan descendants used seals, so Indus-style or Gulf seals in Ur III contexts could belong to such people.
- **Grade:** A, per Vermaak and Parpola et al.

**"(..)-ibra, man of Meluhha"**
- **What it is:** A partly broken name of a Meluhhan leader in a list of rebel kings opposing Naram-Sin ("Great Revolt" tradition). It is cited as Schøyen MS 2814 (Wikipedia).
- **Cuneiform spelling and edition:** UNVERIFIED.
- **Usefulness:** It would be the only royal Meluhhan name, but only "-ibra" survives. It is a later literary (Old Babylonian?) tradition.
- **Grade:** C.

### 2.2 The "Meluhha village" (é-duru5 me-luḫ-ḫa) at Lagash/Girsu (Guabba)
- **Main texts** (Ur III, Girsu, c. Šulgi 34 to Šu-Sin 9; spelling me-luḫ-ḫa throughout):
  - **é-duru5 me-luḫ-ḫa**, "Meluhha village":
    - CT 5 36 = BM 17751 (Šulgi 48)
    - MVN 7 420 = ITT 4 8024 (with Gú-ab-ba{ki}; year "An-ša-an ba-ḫul" = Šulgi 34)
    - SANTAG 7 167
  - **ì-dub é-duru5 me-luḫ-ḫa**, "granary of the Meluhha village": Amherst 54; ASJ 3 152 107 (Amar-Suen 6); MVN 12 371; MVN 13 223 (Umma, Šu-Sin 9); ITT 2 705; TCTI 2 3666; BPOA 2 1881.
  - **giškiri6 me-luḫ-ḫa {d}Nin-MAR.KI**, "Meluhha garden of Ninmar": STA 19 (Šulgi 48).
  - **ugula me-luḫ-ḫa**, "overseer Meluhha": ITT 4 8015 = MVN 7 411.
  - **dumu me-luḫ-ḫa erín é {d}Nanše**, "Meluhhans, workers of the Nanše temple": TUT 154.
- **Source:** Vermaak 2008, "Guabba, the Meluhhan village in Mesopotamia", *Journal for Semitics* 17/2. The PDF header reads pp. 454–471; the BM page cites 553–570, so check the pagination. Also Parpola, Parpola & Brunswig 1977, *JESHO* 20: 129–165, texts 01–11.
- **URLs:**
  - https://www.ancientportsantiques.com/wp-content/uploads/Documents/PLACES/IndOc-Gulf/Lagash-Vermaak2008.pdf
  - https://www.harappa.com/content/guabba-meluhhan-village-mesopotamia
- **Usefulness:** Mainly sociological: a resident Indus-derived community of c. 2060–2030 BC, with links to Ninmar/Nanše temples, gardens, a granary and possibly textiles.
  - It anchors when and where Indus-script users could have produced inscribed objects in Mesopotamia.
  - No Indus inscription is known from Girsu in a matching context, though Laursen's seal no. 22 from Girsu is Indus-inscribed (see 3.3).
- **Grade:** A.

### 2.4 Meluhha as a place name
- **Spellings:** me-luḫ-ḫa{ki} (Sargonic to Ur III); later Akkadian *Meluḫḫa*. In 1st-millennium texts it is re-applied to Nubia/Kush (Amarna letters; Ashurbanipal).
- **Proposed etymologies:** Dravidian *mēl-akam* "high country" (Parpola & Parpola 1975, *Studia Orientalia* 46); a link to Sanskrit *mleccha*.
- **Usefulness:** The name of the land could plausibly appear in Indus texts as an ethnonym or toponym. The phonetic form is too uncertain to pin.
- **Grade:** A for the attestations. C for the etymology.

## PART 3. Indus inscriptions found abroad with datable or interpretable context

### 3.1 Indus seal impression on a jar-mouth sealing, said to be from Umma
- **What it is:** A clay sealing stamped by an inscribed Indus square seal: "bull and manger" motif with a six-sign inscription.
  - The back carries a clear cloth impression from a cloth tied over a jar mouth, with string.
  - It is said to come from "Jokha" (Umma). Tosi says this provenance is unconfirmed.
- **Where it is now:** Ashmolean Museum, Oxford. The Louvre gave it in 1931; it reached the Louvre before 1914. The accession number is UNVERIFIED.
- **Bibliography:**
  - Scheil 1925, *RA* 22: 55–56 (UNVERIFIED pages).
  - Tosi 1991, "The Indus Civilization beyond the Indian subcontinent", in Jansen, Mulloy & Urban (eds.), *Forgotten Cities on the Indus*, 111–128; also reproduced in *Save Mohenjo-daro!* (2022): 56–57.
  - URL: https://www.harappa.com/blog/indus-seal-impression-umma-mesopotamia
- **Usefulness:** HIGH as a type. It is direct proof that an Indus seal text was applied to a container of goods in Mesopotamia.
  - If the contents could be identified (residue analysis), or the jar matched to an Indus jar type known for a commodity, the six-sign sequence could be tested as owner/consignor plus commodity.
  - At present the content is unknown.
- **Grade:** A for the object. B for the provenance.

### 3.2 Indus and Gulf seals from Ur in dated contexts (British Museum / Penn)
- **BM 122187** (reg. 1929,1017.725)
  - Circular glazed steatite seal: bull right, one line of Indus script.
  - Gadd 1932 no. 2; Mitchell 1986 no. 1.
  - Context: Woolley 1928/29, no context date given.
  - URL: https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/W_1929-1017-725
  - Grade: A.

  - Gadd 1932 no. 16; Mitchell 1986 no. 2; Laursen 2010 seal 21.
  - URL: https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/W_1932-1008-178
  - Grade: A.
- **Laursen seal 20, Ur PG 401:** grave dated c. 2200–2000 BC (Nissen). Grade: A (per Laursen).
- **Penn 31-43-74** (U.16397; Gadd no. 11): round seal, scorpion device, "strongly suggests writing".
  - URL: https://www.penn.museum/collections/object/26770
  - Grade: A for the object, C as writing.
- **BM 122947** (U.16220; Gadd no. 6): Dilmun-style cylinder with zebu, scorpion and serpent, from the fill of the Šulgi mausoleum. No inscription.
  - URL: https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/W_1931-1010-15
- **Usefulness:** These are the inscribed Indus-signs objects with the best dates (Ur III, c. 2100–2040 BC), in the same decades as the Meluhha-village texts (2062–2028 BC) and the Urusagrig Meluhhans (Šu-Suen 6).
  - The working hypothesis they allow: inscriptions on Gulf seals from Ur are names of Meluhhan or Dilmunite merchants of the kind recorded in Ur III texts.
  - A direct name-to-seal match has never been made.

### 3.3 "Gulf-type" seals with Indus signs in non-Harappan sequences (key structural anchor)
- **The corpus:** About 27–29 Indus-inscribed Gulf seals, listed in Laursen 2010, Table 1:
  - Bahrain nos. 6–11 and 56
  - Failaka 12–13
  - Susa 14, Luristan 15
  - Ur 16–21 and 24
  - Girsu 22, Babylon 23
  - unprovenanced 25–27
- **Main findings:**
  - Hunter (1932: 469) and Parpola (1994: 309 and passim) found that most of these sign sequences are **not paralleled** in the Indus Valley corpus.
  - Only seals 14 (Susa), 15 (Luristan) and 22 (Girsu) are well paralleled (Laursen 2010: 116).
  - A "twins" sign prefix opens four Bahraini inscriptions (6, 7, 8, 11). That is more cases in initial position than in the whole Indus Valley corpus (Laursen 2010: 117).
  - Seal 11 (Karzakkan) is a pseudo-inscription that still puts "twins" first.
  - Laursen and Parpola conclude that the seals record a **language different from Harappan**, with Indus signs used phonetically or logographically for local, probably personal, names.
- **Source:** Laursen 2010, *Arabian Archaeology and Epigraphy* 21: 96–134.
  - PDF: https://www.harappa.com/sites/default/files/pdf/The_westward_transmission_of_Indus_Valle.pdf
- **Usefulness: HIGH for the project's structural tests.** A corpus of "the same script, a different language" is the closest thing to a natural experiment.
  - If the "twins" or "man" initial sign is a name-class marker (Laursen 2010: 118, "proper noun … most reasonable"), it can be tested against the project's locked positional statistics.
  - Does the "twins" sign behave as a prefix in Indus Valley texts too?
  - Do the non-paralleled Gulf sequences cluster with the name-like segments of Indus texts?
  - This anchors function (name marker), not sound.
- **Grade:** A for the observation. B for the "different language" interpretation.

### 3.4 Other Indus seals in Mesopotamia and Iran with context
- **Susa:**
  - Louvre Sb 2425: Indus-style cylinder/stamp with an elongated buffalo and Indus signs. Its form is described inconsistently in secondary sources.
  - Louvre Sb 5614: round seal impression with buffalo and Indus signs.
  - Context: acropolis, Akkadian to Ur III levels (Amiet).
  - Grade: B (numbers via Wikipedia).

  - No inscription. Grade: A/B.
- **Kish:** an Indus "unicorn" seal found by Mackay (1925, *JRAS*) with etched carnelian beads, and a second seal found by Langdon in a pre-Sargonic context.
  - Museum numbers UNVERIFIED. Grade: B.
- **Nippur:** an Indus square seal in a 14th-century BC Kassite context, probably an heirloom (Gibson 1977; Allchin & Chakrabarti 1997: 560). Grade: B.
- **Lagash/Girsu:** an Indus seal, Genouillac 1930: 27 (= Laursen no. 22?). Grade: B.
- **Latifiya (40 mi S of Baghdad):** a press-seal "possibly of Indus type", found 1931.
  - Wyatt 1983, *JRAS* 115(1): 3–6. Whether it is inscribed is UNVERIFIED. Grade: C.
- **Count:** Vidale 2004: 272, Table 1: Ur 11, Kish 3, Lagash 2, Eshnunna 2, Nippur 1.
- **Usefulness:** These pin Indus inscriptions to a time window (Akkadian to Ur III) and to places where Meluhhans are textually attested. None carries meaning by itself.

### 3.5 Oman (Magan): Ras al-Jinz
- **What it is:** Indus-script-inscribed potsherds and inscribed stamp seals, including a copper stamp seal of Indus shape, at a harbour site of c. 2200–1900 BC.
- **Source:** Cleuziou, Gnoli, Robin & Tosi 1994, "Cachets inscrits de la fin du IIIe millénaire av. notre ère à Ra's al-Junayz, Sultanat d'Oman", *CRAIBL* 138(2): 453–468, DOI 10.3406/crai.1994.15376 (open on Persée).
- **Usefulness:** These are inscriptions on storage jars, in a context of jar trade.
  - Like the Umma sealing, they could link sign groups to vessel contents or capacities if the jars can be typed.
  - Magan is textually attested in cuneiform as a copper source.
- **Grade:** A for the object. C as an anchor.

### 3.6 Metrology: the Dilmun weight standard = the Indus standard
- **The evidence:**
  - Dilmun (Bahrain) used Indus-standard cubic chert weights, with a unit of about 13.6–13.7 g.
  - Old Babylonian cuneiform texts from Ur record a "Dilmun" weight standard, the "stone/mina of Dilmun". The exact text numbers (UET 5) are UNVERIFIED.
- **Sources:**
  - Roaf 1982, "Weights on the Dilmun standard", *Iraq* 44: 137–141.
  - Zaccagnini 1986, "The Dilmun standard and its relationship with Indus and Near Eastern weight systems", *Iraq* 48: 19–23.
- **Usefulness:** Moderate. It gives an exact numerical bridge.
  - If any Indus text records quantities, for example the numeral-plus-sign groups on tablets or tags, the Dilmun/Mesopotamian conversion offers a check on the values of number signs and units.
  - Indus numeral strokes are already accepted as numbers. This could confirm unit or measure signs.
- **Grade:** A for the equivalence of the weight systems. C for any sign link.
