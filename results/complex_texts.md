# Fuls's "complex texts": what are they? (scouting, exploration only)

*21 October 2026. Script: `scripts/complex_texts_scouting.py`. Text classes are Wells's (2011), from the ICIT export's `class` column (`context_scouting.md`): short complex (SC, 606) and long complex (LC, 127) make up 12.9% of ICIT, Fuls's "about 13% complex texts". Fuls (2013, "Positional Analysis of Indus Signs") treats complex texts as exceptions to the normal sign pattern; Wells (1998) suggested short seals may be fragments of longer messages. Exploration texts of 4+ signs; not a confirmatory test.*

## What complex texts are

| Texts of 4+ signs | Heading first | Jar G740 anywhere | Numeral in the last two signs | Numeral first |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Complex | **11%** | **24%** | **26%** | 18% |
| Patterned | 51% | 70% | 11% | 5% |
| Single segment | 12% | 62% | 9% | 21% |
| All seals | 36% | 58% | 18% | 10% |

- They **drop the frame**: no heading in 89%, no jar in 76%.
- Half still end with a closer (G740 17%, G400 9%, G151, G520, G617); the other half end with signs that are rarely final elsewhere: G368, G390, G590 (final 9% of the time elsewhere), numerals G31/G1/G33, even the heading sign G861 (final 6% elsewhere).
- **Numbers drift to the end**: a numeral in the last two signs in 26% of complex texts vs 11% of patterned ones.

**Reading.** Complex texts are not damaged, reversed, misordered or joined versions of normal texts. They look like a **distinct, frame-less formula** on otherwise ordinary seals: no heading, usually no jar, with counts placed late where the jar would stand. Wells's fragment idea does not explain them either: fragments of a framed message would keep part of the frame, and here the frame is mostly gone.

**Next.** Whether complex texts share a vocabulary with each other (a sub-system) more than with patterned texts; whether they cluster in time (Harappa periods) or find spots. Prior work: Wells (2011) defines the classes; his and Fuls's discussion of complex texts should be read before claiming novelty.
