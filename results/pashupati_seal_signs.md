# The Pashupati seal's seven signs: what they do across the corpus

*Descriptive and exploratory, 14 October 2026. No hypothesis was tested on the holdout. The seal itself is in the exploration split.*

## Which seal

The "Pashupati" or "proto-Shiva" seal is Mackay's seal 420 from Mohenjo-daro (steatite, about 3.5 cm, excavated 1928–29, now in the National Museum, Delhi), with seven signs above a seated horned figure surrounded by animals ([Pashupati seal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pashupati_seal)). In the CISI catalogue it is **M-304** ([Sengupta 2023, figure caption](https://www.researchgate.net/figure/Two-seals-from-Mohenjo-Daro-depicting-seated-deities-with-three-faces-visible-a-CISI_fig7_375748777); [paper listing M-304 and M-305 as the Pashupati seals](https://www.academia.edu/106219345/Pashupati_seals_of_Indus_Valley_Civilisation_and_Shiva_of_Puranas)).

**Catalogue trap.** "420" is Mackay's number. In our corpus, CISI **M-420** is a different object: a three-sign inscription on pottery. Matching by the wrong catalogue number would have analysed the wrong object.

In our full corpus, M-304 is text `S2823`: a steatite seal with motif "Scene", seven signs. In reading order (the dataset's convention; see METHODS):

| Position | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 6 | 7 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Sign | G90 | G550 | G534 | G742 | G220 | G740 | G90 |
| Shape | standing man | crossed-triangle sign | similar, with inner strokes | jar with marks | fish | jar | standing man |
| CISI P-number (crosswalk) | P013 | P230 | none | P326 (weak) | P050 | P324 | P013 |

**Caution on order.** The seventh sign on the seal is pushed down below the line for lack of space ([Pashupati seal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pashupati_seal)). Where a transcriber places that sign is a judgement, so the two G90s at both ends may partly reflect transcription. The same man sign appearing twice is as transcribed.

## What each sign does elsewhere

## What this shows

1. **The seal ends with an ordinary closing formula.** Its last three signs, fish–jar–man, occur together in **12 distinct texts** in the exploration split alone, and jar–man in **52**. These are mostly everyday seals, many with the unicorn. The Pashupati inscription closes the way dozens of ordinary seals close.
2. **Most of its signs are common, formulaic signs.** Jar, fish and man are among the most patterned signs in the corpus, each with a fixed job: ending, being counted, closing after the jar.
3. **Only the middle is unusual.** G534 occurs twice in the whole corpus, and G550–G534–G742 occurs nowhere else. If anything on this seal is specific to it, it is in signs 2 to 4, which are too rare to analyse statistically.

So the inscription looks like **a standard seal legend** (an opening, a particular middle, a closing formula shared with ordinary seals) rather than a unique sentence describing the scene below it. That fits the general picture from results 8: inscriptions built from fixed opening and closing phrases, as names, titles or labels tend to be.

## Against the published readings

- **The picture.** Marshall's proto-Shiva, Srinivasan's buffalo-man deity, Hiltebeitel's Mahishasura prototype and Witzel's "Lord of the Animals" are readings of the **image**, not the text ([Pashupati seal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pashupati_seal)). The sign data neither supports nor refutes them. They do show that the text is not tailored to the scene: its closing formula appears on seals with other animals.
- **Readings of the text.** Parpola reads the inscription as naming a servant of an aquatic deity he links to Varuna; Fairservis read the figure as "Anil," chief of four clans (as summarised in [Pashupati seal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pashupati_seal)). Any reading must also work on the other 12 texts ending fish–jar–man and the 52 ending jar–man. A reading that makes those words specific to this god or this scene does not fit.
- **Other readings circulating online** (sentences about traps and fish, or "chief of the mountain") could not be traced to a citable source for this note and are not attributed here. The same test applies to them.

**Why a single seal can't be deciphered.** Seven signs allow almost any story. Four different confident readings of the same seven signs is what that looks like. What can be tested is how each sign behaves across thousands of texts, as above.

## Possible next step (not done)

A pre-registrable hypothesis this suggests: "Texts with a scene or deity motif use the same closing formulas (jar; jar–man; fish–jar–man) as ordinary unicorn seals at the same rate." Too few scene seals may exist in the holdout to test it; the count should be checked first.
