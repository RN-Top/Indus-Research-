## Claim 1: Corner or enclosing strokes make a sign appear at the end of an inscription

**Verdict: ANTICIPATED (qualitatively).** Hunter (1934) and Mahadevan (1986) said this, as reported by Yadav & Vahia (2011). I found no statistical or held-out test of it, so the project's test is new as a test, but the observation is not.

- **Yadav, N. & Vahia, M. N. (2011). "Indus Script: A Study of its Sign Design." *SCRIPTA* 3: 1–36.** https://www.harappa.com/sites/default/files/pdf/Indus-sign-design.pdf
  - p. 11 (Table 2): modifier **906 = "four vertical strokes enclosing the sign"**. Also 913 = four *pairs* of strokes enclosing; 916 = three pairs plus one stroke enclosing.
  - p. 26: "An indication of the possible logic is also seen in the usage of another modifier 906 (Hunter, 1934; Mahadevan, 1986). They have suggested that 906 seems to replace frequent text ender signs such as sign number 211 or 342 … indicating that addition of the modifier 906 to a sign makes it a pseudo ender."
  - p. 26 also says modifier 905 (a stroke at the top) moves signs 342 and 328 *toward the beginning* of texts. This is a related positional effect of a modifier.
  - Only sign 61 (= 59 + 197 + 906) is named explicitly. Table 2 counts 906 as occurring 20 times.
- **Hunter, G. R. (1934)** and **Mahadevan, I. (1986)**, as cited above. Primary pages were not verified.
- **Parpola (1988/1994)** and Mahadevan's review of Parpola discuss "diacritic-like marks" on signs, but in the parts I read they say nothing about position. Mahadevan's review (https://www.harappa.com/script/maha15.html) quotes Parpola's "present inability to identify morphological markers with any certainty".
- **Daggumati & Revesz (2021)**, HSSC 8:50 (https://www.nature.com/articles/s41599-021-00713-0): treats stroke differences as allographs. It does not link enclosing strokes to final position.
- Kak, Rao, Fairservis, Bonta, Possehl, Koskenniemi, Wells, Fuls: **not found** making this specific claim, in what I could access. Fuls 2024 ("Comparison of Linear Elamite and Indus Writing Systems", *Iranian J. Archaeol. Studies*) lists "enclosing by strokes" as a sign-modification type. I could not read its position claims.
- **Suggested wording:** "Hunter (1934) and Mahadevan (1986) noted (cited in Yadav & Vahia 2011: 26) that the four-stroke enclosure modifier appears to turn a sign into a 'pseudo-ender'. We test this statistically on held-out data."

## Claim 2: Copper tablets pair the same text with the same image

**Verdict: ANTICIPATED.** Parpola wrote about this repeatedly, from 1975 onward. Mahadevan's group also stated it in 2009. Note the known "two different motifs, same text" sets, which are a structured exception.

- **Parpola, A. (2009). "'Hind Leg' + 'Fish': Towards Further Understanding of the Indus Script." *SCRIPTA* 1: 37–76.** https://www.harappa.com/sites/default/files/pdf/script-indus-parpola.pdf
  - p. 51: the 240 copper tablets show "a clear interdependence between the inscription on the obverse and the iconographic motif on the reverse". The tablets form sets of duplicates, and "In some sets, a single sign on the reverse has the same inscription on the obverse" as another set whose reverse shows a figure. "It appears as if the single sign stood for the name of the divinity illustrated."
  - Footnote 32 points to earlier statements: **Parpola 1975a: 196–199; Parpola 1994: 110–112, 234 (fig. 13.13); Parpola 2008b.**
- **Ganesan, S., Subramanian, C., Babu, S., Nair, S. & Mahadevan, I. (2009). "The Indus Script: Text and Context: A Statistical-Positional Analysis of Significant Text Segments." *Bulletin of the Indus Research Centre* No. 1 (2008–09), RMRL Chennai.** https://rmrl.in/bulletin/bulletin-No-1-Sept-2009.pdf
  - p. 6, §7.1(7): "However, one notable exception is the association of significant text segments with particular field symbols on the copper tablets, (e.g. texts with the field symbol 'hare facing a bush')." §7(5): "The FS=16 (Hare) occurs only with the following triplets".
- **Mahadevan, review of Parpola 1994** (https://www.harappa.com/script/maha15.html; also https://www.harappa.com/node/3134) describes "two sets of copper tablets, both with the same inscription on one side but two different motifs on the other": the "fig tree + crab" sign versus the horned archer. **This is a counter-case:** one text goes with two images, but one image is a sign that reads as the other image.
- **Ruzsa, F. (INDOLOGY list, 7 Aug 2000)** (https://list.indology.info/pipermail/indology/2000-August/022316.html): "the really long series are from tablets: copper in Mohenjo-daro". This is about duplicate series and does not address image pairing.
- Earlier background: Mackay 1931 (in Marshall), "Seals, seal impressions, and copper tablets, with tabulation", pp. 370–405, and Mackay 1938. These give the original tabulation. I did not check whether they state the pairing.

## Claim 3: Layout (opening cluster + middle + terminal jar) predicts object type (seal vs tablet vs other)

**Verdict: PARTLY anticipated.** People have shown that particular segments are tied to object type, and Rao has proposed a component grammar in which the terminal marks the object's function. I found no classifier that predicts object type from layout, and one study reports *no* significant link between text clusters and object type.

- **Ganesan et al. (2009)**, bulletin as above:
  - §6.2.1: "Thus it appears that the main cause of deviation in these cases is the object type and not the site." One sign "is ranked first on miniature tablets".
  - §7.1(2): an initial pair "occurs mostly in the initial position with a frequency of 254 out of 291, mostly on seals and sealings … does not occur on copper tablets".
  - §7.1(4): a triplet occurs "mostly on miniature tablets and never on copper tablets or pottery".
- **Rao, R. P. N. (2017). "The Indus Script and Economics: A Role for Indus Seals and Tablets in Rationing and Administration of Labor." In Frenez et al. (eds), *Walking with the Unicorn*, Archaeopress, pp. 518–525.** https://www.harappa.com/sites/default/files/pdf/The-Indus-Script.pdf (also arXiv:1812.00049)
  - pp. 522–523 give the structure "Prefix: Institution/Business/Landlord/Family/Person; Medial: Number/Quantity/Measures; Core: Commodity; Terminal: Function of sealing/tablet".
  - p. 523: "the 'Terminal' component may indicate the overall function of the token, sealing or tablet". This is the closest anticipation of the structure itself. It is a hypothesis, not a tested result.
- **Yadav, N. (2019). "Structure of Indus Script." *Indian J. History of Science* 54(2): 125–134.** https://cahc.jainuniversity.ac.in/assets/ijhs/Vol54_2_2019__Art01.pdf
  - p. 130: "The text clusters were not found to have any significant correlation to the sites of discovery or object types." **This contradicts the claim. Address it.**
  - p. 131: "in terms of usage of signs, seals and sealings are quite different".
- **Fuls, A. (2023). *A Catalog of Indus Signs*** (https://www.academia.edu/103538728) has appendices on sign usage by artefact type. Not read in full.
- **Vidale 2007** (cited by Nair 2026, arXiv:2604.17828) is cited for the observation that "sign usage varies across artifact classes". Not read.
- **Mukhopadhyay 2023** (HSSC) discusses "typological and functional differences between the seals and tablets" (from the abstract). Its layout-based claims were not verified.

## Claim 4: Seal text is independent of the animal emblem

**Verdict: ANTICIPATED as an observation.** Ganesan et al. 2009 state it explicitly, and Farmer and Ruzsa documented same-text/different-animal cases in 2000. **No predictive test** (can the text predict the animal on held-out data?) was found. The project's quantitative test is new, but the conclusion matches theirs.

- **Ganesan et al. (2009)**, p. 6, §7.1(7): "The significant text segments do not appear to have special affinity to field symbols with which they are associated on seals and sealings. This is also shown by the simple observation that the same text occurs with different field symbols and vice versa."
  - Partial exceptions: §6.3, one sign "occurs most frequently with FS=14 (Ox-Antelope)". §7.1(2): "greater affinity … towards MD along with the field symbol Unicorn".
- **Farmer, S. (INDOLOGY, 6 Aug 2000)** (https://list.indology.info/pipermail/indology/2000-August/022273.html): "the same inscriptions are sometimes associated with different animal icons" (Marshall seals #252 and #378). He also refers to the "much more common 'same inscription/same animal' perforated seals". In an 8 Aug post (…/022307.html) he tested a "same animal/same inscription" rule.
- **Ruzsa, F. (INDOLOGY, 7 Aug 2000)** (…/022316.html) lists four texts, "always on seals", each appearing "with the 'unicorn', but also with some other animal as well".
- **Frenez, D. (2017). "Private Person or Public Persona? …" In *Walking with the Unicorn*, pp. 166–188.** https://www.harappa.com/sites/default/files/pdf/private-person.pdf
  - p. 172: "the animals and the other images represented on the standard Indus seals probably had a function completely or partially separate from the inscription". This is an argument, not a statistical test.
- **Rao (2017)**, p. 524, Fig. 7: two seals "with 'unicorn' and zebu bull motifs and a 'numeral + grain stalk' inscription". This is an example of the same text type appearing with different animals.
- **Mahadevan 1977** (*Texts, Concordance and Tables*) was the "first to study the field symbols, and their relationship with inscription, object types and sites" (C. Subramanian 2025 lecture, slide 19). The tables were not read.
- **Mukhopadhyay 2019** (Palgrave Comms 5:73): in the text I read, I found nothing on text versus animal. **Mukhopadhyay 2023** argues the iconographies "might have been the emblems of the guilds, rulers, and/or governing bodies". She did not test text against animal, as far as I could read.

## Claim 5: Texts without the opening cluster begin with rare signs (personal names)

**Verdict: PARTLY anticipated.** The general idea that seal texts contain names and titles, and that a "prefix" segment names a person or institution, is old: Parpola, Mahadevan, Rao. **The specific distributional claim was not found:** no one, as far as I could find, says that texts lacking the common opening cluster start with rare signs. The best-supported 2023 work argues *against* names.

- **Parpola, A. (1994). *Deciphering the Indus Script*,** Introduction, p. ~2: "the signs on Indus seals probably show the names and titles of their owners".
- **Parpola, A. (2010), Coimbatore lecture "A Dravidian solution to the Indus script problem"** (https://tamilnet.com/img/publish/2014/11/Parpola-2010-Coimbatore.pdf), p. 15: seal inscriptions "chiefly contain proper names of persons with or without their occupational or official titles".
- **Mahadevan, review of Parpola** (harappa.com/script/maha15.html): "Granting that the seal-texts are probably only strings of names and titles".
- **Rao (2017)**, p. 523: "We hypothesize that the Prefix may denote a person, business, landlord, family, or institution". He does not say prefixes are rare signs.
- **Mahadevan & Bhaskar (2018). "Toponyms, Directions and Tribal Names in the Indus Script."** In *Walking with the Unicorn* (https://www.harappa.com/sites/default/files/pdf/tribal-names-indus-script.pdf):
  - p. 375: sign 267 is the "most frequent initial sign"; frequency "Initial: 298. Total: 376". They read it as a place/citadel.
  - p. 366: "Personal nouns are formed by the addition of Gender-Number suffixes to toponyms". They read the jar as "Masculine singular suffix".
  - This places names in the *initial* segment, but as common place signs, not rare signs.
- **Yadav et al. (2010), PLoS ONE 5(3): e9506** (https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0009506): "text enders being more strictly defined than text beginners" and "Text beginners are more selective in terms of the number of signs which can follow them". This is about beginner flexibility, not rarity or names.
- **Counter-position: Mukhopadhyay 2023** (HSSC 10; https://www.academia.edu/111920219) argues "the inscriptions did not encode any proper noun, such as anthroponyms, toponyms, or names of specific organizations" and "Indus-inscriptions did not encode names of individual seal-owners". **Cite this and respond to it if you claim personal names.**
- Farmer (2000) used same-text/different-animal duplicates as an argument against texts being "ID tags" or personal names.

## One-line verdicts
1. Corner/enclosing strokes give final position: **Anticipated.** Hunter 1934 and Mahadevan 1986 ("pseudo-ender", modifier 906), via Yadav & Vahia 2011: 26. No prior statistical test found.
2. Copper tablets pair same text with same image: **Anticipated.** Parpola 1975a, 1994: 110–112, 2008, 2009: 51; Ganesan et al. 2009 §7.1(7). Mahadevan notes the two-motif exception.
3. Layout predicts object type: **Partly.** Segment–object links appear in Ganesan et al. 2009; Rao 2017 hypothesizes "Terminal = function of sealing/tablet". Yadav 2019 found no cluster–object correlation. No predictive test found.
4. Text is independent of the animal emblem: **Anticipated (observationally).** Ganesan et al. 2009 §7.1(7); Farmer and Ruzsa 2000; Frenez 2017: 172. No predictive or held-out test found.
5. Texts without the opening cluster begin with rare signs (names): **Partly.** The names/titles idea is in Parpola 1994 and 2010, Mahadevan, and Rao 2017. The specific rare-initial-sign pattern was not found. Mukhopadhyay 2023 argues against names.
