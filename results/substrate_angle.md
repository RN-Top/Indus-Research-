# The substrate angle: echoes of the Indus language in later languages

*Literature review and assessment, 14 October 2026. Not a holdout test. Speculation is labelled as such.*

**Bottom line.** Scholars broadly agree that the earliest Sanskrit borrowed a few hundred words from a language or languages already spoken in the north-west, and that some river names there are older than Sanskrit. Which language that was, and whether it was the Indus language, is still argued, with Dravidian, a lost Munda-like language and an unknown "Language X" all proposed. None of this currently yields a prediction about our Indus corpus that could tell the hypotheses apart, because testing a language against the signs needs sign sound-values first, and the borrowed words are mostly names for plants, animals and tools rather than the grammar a sign corpus can show. Two weaker structural tests are proposed at the end.

## 1. Non-Sanskrit words in early Sanskrit

**How many.** Kuiper identified 383 words in the Rigveda as non-Indo-Aryan, about 4% of its vocabulary; Oberlies (1994) prefers 344 to 358 secure cases; Witzel (2000) estimates 211 to 250 foreign words excluding local names, about 2% ([summary with sources](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Substratum_in_Vedic_Sanskrit)).

**Witzel's "Para-Munda" (Kubhā-Vipāś) substrate.** In [Witzel, "Substrate Languages in Old Indo-Aryan," *EJVS* 5-1 (1999), pp. 1–67](https://www.academia.edu/713996), Witzel argues that the foreign words of the earliest Rigveda point to a western Austro-Asiatic (Munda-related) language in the Punjab, which he suggests was the language of the Indus period. His evidence:

- Many borrowed words carry prefixes typical of Austro-Asiatic, such as *ka-*, *ki-*, *ku-*.
- Dravidian loans are absent from the earliest Rigveda and appear only in its middle and later layers.
- Much agricultural vocabulary has no Dravidian explanation.

Example words he discusses (his proposed sources, paraphrased): *vrīhi* rice, *lāṅgala* plough, *mayūra* peacock, and the river names *Sutudrī* (Sutlej), *Vipāś* (Beas) and *Kubhā*. He later renamed the language the "Kubhā-Vipāś substrate" to avoid implying it was Munda itself ([summary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Substratum_in_Vedic_Sanskrit)).

**Other proposals.** Masica (1979) could not trace 31% of agricultural and plant terms and proposed an unknown "Language X." Southworth (1979) found only five plant terms shared with Munda and inferred further, now-lost languages. Zvelebil (1990) proposed Dravidian sources for words such as *kulāya* nest, *daṇḍa* stick and *khala* threshing floor; Mallory and Adams (1997) estimate 30 to 40 Dravidian loans in Vedic ([summary with sources](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Substratum_in_Vedic_Sanskrit)).

**Counter-arguments.** Thieme criticised an over-eager hunt for Dravidian loans; Das (1995) argued that no Rigvedic word has an agreed foreign origin (Kuiper replied in 1995); Krishnamurti (2003) faulted Witzel's method for showing few complete Munda words; Anderson (2008) notes the surprising absence of Munda plant and animal borrowings; Hock (2015) argues that retroflex consonants, often cited as Dravidian influence, are an areal feature ([summary with sources](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Substratum_in_Vedic_Sanskrit)).

## 2. River and place names

- [Blažek, "Hydronymia R̥gvedica," *Linguistica Brunensia* 64(2) (2016): 7–54](https://digilib2.phil.muni.cz/en/node/27489) analysed 29 Rigvedic river names. Most can be explained as Indo-Aryan at first approximation, though some of these may be later reinterpretations. Burushaski origins look more likely for 2 (6.9%) and Dravidian alternatives are possible for 4 (13.8%); in all, about one fifth have a more or less probable pre-Indo-Aryan origin.
- On *Sindhu* (the Indus itself), Witzel ([INDOLOGY list, 24 Oct 1999](https://list.indology.info/pipermail/indology/1999-October/018781.html)) notes a good Indo-European etymology but also a possible older local word, *\*sind-/send-*, attested from Hunza to Sindh and in Mesopotamian references to Meluhhan goods under forms like *sinda*. He calls this preliminary.
- On name movement, Witzel ([INDOLOGY list, 8 Sep 1999](https://list.indology.info/pipermail/indology/1999-September/018366.html)) argues that several river names moved from Afghanistan into South Asia rather than the reverse, and that *Kubhā* is probably a loan.

These are mailing-list posts, informal by the author's own description; they are cited for his stated views, not as peer-reviewed findings.

## 3. Brahui

Brahui is a Dravidian language spoken in Balochistan, far from other Dravidian languages ([summary with sources](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brahvis)).

- **Late migration.** Elfenbein (1987, 1989) argues that Brahui speakers arrived from central India within the last two thousand years, probably after 1000 CE: Brahui has no Old Iranian loanwords, and it shares sound changes with Kurukh and Malto of central and eastern India (Krishnamurti 2003).
- **Relic.** Others treat Brahui as a remnant of a once-wider Dravidian presence; Parpola (1994) links it to Harappan culture.
- **Genetics.** Pagani et al. (2017) find Brahui ancestry closer to neighbouring populations than to Dravidian speakers of India, which the authors read as support for the relic view; Singh et al. (2025) find no recent shared ancestry with their closest linguistic relatives.

The question is open. Even if Brahui is a relic, it does not show that the Indus language was Dravidian.

## 4. Was the Indus language Dravidian?

**For.** Parpola builds his partial readings on Proto-Dravidian rebus values, for example fish = *mīn*, also "star", and six + fish = *aṟu-mīn*, the Old Tamil name of the Pleiades ([Parpola, ICES lecture, 2005, pp. 52–54](https://www.harappa.com/sites/default/files/pdf/indusscript(2).pdf); [Parpola, *SCRIPTA* 1, 2009](https://www.harappa.com/sites/default/files/pdf/script-indus-parpola.pdf)). He links the name Meluhha to a Dravidian form (as reported in an [INDOLOGY post, 8 Jan 2000](https://list.indology.info/pipermail/indology/2000-January/019737.html)).

**Against or unresolved.**
- Witzel's evidence that Dravidian loans appear only in the later Rigveda points to a different language in the Punjab at the time (above).
- Parpola himself calls his work a "limited decipherment" of about two dozen signs, limited by how little Proto-Dravidian vocabulary can be reconstructed (*SCRIPTA* 2009, p. 61).
- [Farmer, Sproat and Witzel, "The Collapse of the Indus-Script Thesis," *EJVS* 11-2 (2004): 19–57](https://list.indology.info/pipermail/indology/2004-December/028822.html) argue that the signs may not encode language at all, which would make every language hypothesis moot. [Rao and colleagues reply](https://homes.cs.washington.edu/~rao/IndusCompLing.html) that the signs' combined properties make language more likely, without claiming proof.

## 5. Can any of this be tested against our corpus?

**Not directly, and here is why.**

1. **Testing a language needs sound values first.** Borrowed words such as *lāṅgala* plough or *vrīhi* rice could only be found in the inscriptions by first assigning sounds to signs, which is the decipherment itself. Any test would be circular.
2. **The borrowed words are content words.** They name crops, animals, tools and rivers. The patterns a sign corpus shows clearly, such as positions, recurring units and endings, reflect grammar and formulas, which these word lists do not cover.
3. **The texts are short and formulaic.** About five signs on average, with fixed opening and closing phrases (our results 8 and 9). That suits names and titles, which carry little grammar.
4. **Competing hypotheses predict similar sign statistics.** Dravidian is suffixing; Munda languages use both prefixes and suffixes. Without a known comparison corpus written the same way, edge statistics cannot say which family produced them.

**Two weaker structural tests (speculative; not yet registered).** These test premises shared by language-based readings, not any one language:

- **S1. Do the ending signs behave like grammatical suffixes?** If the jar sign (G740) is a suffix, as Dravidian-based readings assume, it should follow a much wider variety of signs than frequency-matched signs do. This cannot say which language, and a non-linguistic "end of label" marker would pass too.
- **S2. Is there a prefix-like class at the start?** A prefixing language would predict a small set of signs that open many different units and are followed by a wide variety of signs. Our opening formulas are fixed phrases, which may defeat this test.

**Things that could become decisive with new evidence:**
- A verified list of Meluhhan personal names in cuneiform sources, matched to the recurring units found on Indus seals (results 8).
- A bilingual inscription, or Indus seals from Mesopotamia and the Gulf with recorded owners.
- An independently compiled dictionary of reconstructed Proto-Dravidian or Munda forms, used to score sound keys in the Reading Tester without the circularity described in Phase 2.
