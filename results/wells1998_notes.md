# Wells 1998 (MA thesis): what it already says, set against this project's results

*Reading notes, 19 October 2026 (first pass), with a second, item-by-item pass the same day (section "Second pass" below, which supersedes the table where they differ). The thesis PDF was sent by Andreas Fuls. It is not copied into this repository; page numbers refer to the thesis. These are notes, not a test. Our counts quoted here are from the exploration split only.*

**Source:** Bryan K. Wells (1998), MA thesis, *An Introduction to Indus Writing*. Chapter 4, "The Structure of the Indus Inscriptions", including **Column Analysis** and **Figure 4.6** (the page Fuls cites).

**Sign numbers:** the 1998 thesis uses Wells's *own early sign numbers*, not the later G-numbers in our corpus. For example, his 1998 sign 288 is the "jar", our G740. Any number below written "sign 288" is his 1998 number. A concordance would be needed for exact matching.

## What Wells did

- **Column analysis.** He took the 327 Mohenjo-daro seal inscriptions containing his sign 288, the jar, and placed signs in columns by their relative left or right order.
- **Fields.** The columns group into **three fields (I–III) and eight sub-fields (A–H)**. Under a Proto-Dravidian Subject–Object–Verb assumption he reads them as subject, object and verb.
- **Short seals as fragments.** Seals of 5 signs or fewer are treated as fragments of longer messages, meant to be combined through multiple seal impressions on tags.

## Point by point against our results

| Our result | What Wells 1998 says | Assessment |
|---|---|---|
| Heading → middle → closer layout (confirmed on the holdout) | Fields I → II → III from column analysis | **Anticipated.** Fuls is right. Our contribution is the pre-registered, cross-site, held-out test. |
| Openers followed by G2 or G60 (`heading_types.md`) | Field I "is defined as the sign clusters which end with the markers" 193, 196 and 231. He notes a fixed sequence: 571 + 231 is always followed by 290, 291 or 292. Signs 341, 342, 543 and 571 "often collocate with markers". | **Largely anticipated:** marker signs close the opening cluster, and some pairings are fixed. *Not* found in this reading: an explicit statement that particular openers take one marker and never the other, or a test of it. Do not claim the pairing as new without checking Figure 4.6 and the later work (Wells 2006, 2011). |
| G740, the "jar", as the generic closer | Sign 288 (the jar) ends Field III; he reads it as a "verbal case marker" | **Same observation, different interpretation.** Our functional reading treats it as a "belongs to" mark, like Mesopotamian *arad₂-zu*. Wells reads it as a Dravidian verbal ending. Neither is proven. |
| Numerals plus count-like items in the middle (`number_system_scouting.md`) | Numerals are "most commonly found in Field II", read as the objects, "possibly the names of commodities of trade". | Specific closers on object classes (G400 tablets; G407/G617 copper tablets) | Not covered in the parts read. He notes copper tablets were handled separately in his sign generation. | Apparently new as tested claims; check Wells 2006/2011. |
| The end-of-middle slot (G100, G760, G142, G923) | Sub-fields and "suffix" columns: "column 8 (Field II) and columns 14, 15 and 16 (Field III)" act as suffixes | **Partly anticipated:** suffix positions are described. |

| Fuls's ~13% "complex" texts | Not addressed directly in the parts read. Wells's view that short seals are *fragments* of longer messages is relevant: fragments would often lack a full frame. | Worth testing: are complex texts more often on seals likely to have been used together? |

## Takeaways

1. **Cite Wells (1998), Chapter 4 and Figure 4.6,** for the field structure, the markers that close the opening cluster, and suffix columns. The project's claims should be framed as *tests* of Wells's structure, plus the object-class closers, the rare-first-sign result and the cross-script comparisons.
2. **The heading-families result is very likely anticipated** by Wells's marker analysis. Treat it as confirmation unless the exact opener-to-marker exclusivity turns out not to be in Wells 1998, 2006 or 2011.
3. **Wells's interpretive layer** (Proto-Dravidian subject–object–verb, verbal case endings) is an assumption. This project stays neutral on language.

### Results, one by one

| Our result | What the thesis actually shows | Verdict |
|---|---|---|
| **Layout** heading → middle → closer | Fields I → II → III. But Wells says his column regularity "breaks down when it is extended to inscriptions from Harappa, or from other types of artifacts" (p. 116). | **Anticipated** for Mohenjo-daro seals. Our held-out test that the coarse layout **does** carry to other sites (76% against 27%) goes beyond what he claimed, and partly against his caution. |
| **Heading families**: oval openers + G2; G920 / G692 + G60 | Field I is closed by the markers 193 (G2), 196 and 231 (G60). Tables 4.1–4.2 show the ovals 341/342 (G817/G820) followed by 193 (G2). Figure 4.6 splits sub-field A into three sub-columns headed by the three markers. "Where Field I consists of 231 + 571 [G60 + G920] the following sign is always 290, 291 or 292" (p. 98). | **Anticipated in substance.** Our data agree: G920 + G60 is followed by a marked jar (G741/G742/G745) in 27 of 37 texts (73%; 29 counting the plain jar). Wells says "always" on his Mohenjo-daro sample. |
| — *within that*: what may be new | Not stated in the parts read: (1) G817 and G861 take **only** G2 (67/78 and 73/83; never G60 or the single stroke); (2) G820 takes all three markers (G2 56, G60 15, single stroke 10); (3) **G692 + G60 is never followed by a marked jar** (0 of 19), so the "G60 family" is two different things. | Point (3) is the one clean addition. The rest is a quantification of what Wells drew. Figure 4.6 places some oval shapes under the hooked-stroke sub-column; whether these are G817/G861 or other ovals cannot be told from the drawing. |
| **End-of-middle slot** (G100, G760, G142, G923; 90% on the holdout) | Column 12, the last column before the jar (column 13), holds man figures, pronged jars, brackets and many others. | **Anticipated as a position.** Wells's column 12 is broad. Our narrow four-sign set and its held-out rate are a sharper, tested form of it. |
| **G740 as generic closer** | 288 ends Field III; read as a verbal case marker | Same observation, different interpretation (unchanged). |
| **Numerals; count-like vs formula-like items** | Numerals mostly in Field II (commodities). Table 4.2 shows that 199 (two strokes) is a marker in one place and a numeral in another, decided by context. | Numerals in the middle: **anticipated.** The split into count-like items (fish, G390, G900) and fixed number + sign formulas (G520, G585, G803), and the 7 / 12 peaks of stacked strokes: **not found in Wells 1998.** |
| **Object-class closers** (G400 on Harappa tablets; G407/G617 on copper tablets) | General statement: "inscriptions from different classes of artifacts have different sign sequences and inventories" (p. 83). Copper tablets are discussed for sign–picture substitution (the hare tablets with sign 338), not for their closing sign. | **The general idea is Wells's.** The specific closers, tested on held-out texts, are **not in Wells 1998.** |
| **Rare first signs in heading-less texts** (32% against 17%) | Field I may mark "names, titles and locatives" (p. 98). | **Proto-Elamite and Mesopotamian comparisons** | Figure 4.2 compares sign *functions* in four ancient scripts in general terms. No statistical comparison. | **Not in Wells 1998.** |

### Revised takeaways

1. **Wells 1998 had the layout, the markers, the G920 + G60 → marked-jar sequence, the pre-jar column and numerals in the middle.** Our versions of these are tests and quantifications of his structure, and should be cited that way.
2. **What this project adds, as far as the 1998 thesis goes:**
   - held-out, pre-registered tests, including the layout across sites, which Wells doubted;
   - G692 + G60 behaving differently from G920 + G60;
   - object-specific closers (G400, G407/G617);
   - the number-system split;
   - the rare-first-sign result;
   - the overseas-sign network and the cross-script comparisons.
3. **Still unchecked:** Wells's later books may already contain some of point 2:
   - the 2006 Harvard dissertation;
   - *Epigraphic Approaches to Indus Writing* (Oxbow, 2011);
   - *The Archaeology and Epigraphy of Indus Writing* (Archaeopress, 2015).
