# Wells 2006 (PhD thesis): what it already says, set against this project's results

*21 October 2026. Bryan K. Wells (2006), *Epigraphic Approaches to the Indus Script*, PhD dissertation, Harvard University (published 2011 as *Epigraphic Approaches to Indus Writing*, Oxbow). Read from the Internet Archive scan (archive.org item `epigraphicapproachestoinduswritingbryankennethwellsphd.thesis_230_Z`), supplied by Erin; not copied here. Page numbers are the thesis's printed numbers. Fuls pointed Erin to Wells 2011 for the ICIT iconography codes.*

## The ICIT iconography codes (pp. 5–9, 24–30)

- **Field symbols (Table 1.2, p. 5; Figure 1.2, p. 7).** Bull:i = unicorn bull. "Bull:i body markings": M = Mask, I, II, O, Q, S, T, J, U, V, **W**, X = Other; each drawn in Figure 1.2. W is the double-loop marking on the shoulder and body (the shape of a capital W, as in Wells 1998, p. 28).
- **Cult objects (Figure 1.4, p. 9; p. 24).** Standards have a top (S square, H half-round, R round), a bottom (A–E) and frills (N none, F frills); e.g. **SAN** = square top, type A bottom, no frills; **SAF** = the same with frills.
- **Table 1.3 (p. 25), 810 unicorns with standards:** W is the commonest marking (44.8%); SAN the commonest standard (66.3%). **SAF goes with W 11.23 of 14.32 points (78%)**; standards with Type 10 bottoms go with W 95% of the time (p. 28).
- Wells: the pairings of standards and markings "are not simply random variations, but are linked to unknown cultural practices" (p. 29); Field Symbols and Cult Objects "represent different classes of groups (for example, political vs. economic vs. religious)" (p. 30).
- **Text and iconography (p. 30):** "There is no noticeable connection between iconography and Indus texts. Specific signs are not associated consistently or exclusively with specific iconographic elements."

## Text classes (pp. 133–148, Table 4.4)

- Five sets: Segment (SS, MS), Patterned (SP, LP), Complex (SC 3–5 signs, LC 6+), Too short / incomplete, Sign 700 + numeral (VN).
- **Complex texts (p. 144):** "although they use the same sign inventories as other inscriptions, the signs are arranged in a completely unique sequences when compared to segmented and patterned texts." Short complex texts occur on all artefact types; long complex texts are commonest on TAB:C (copper tablets) at Mohenjo-daro. Wells: complex texts are important "because of the possibility that they are syllabic spellings" (p. 145; analysed in his Chapter 6).
- **Single segment texts (pp. 135–137, Fig. 4.6):** may be parts of longer messages combined in use; multiple segment texts often separated by sign 001.

## Initial clusters (pp. 138–141, Table 4.5)

- Two-sign initial clusters with the markers ″ (G2), ' (G1) and ꞉ (G60): the oval openers take ″ (143 and 113 cases); others take ″, ' or the hooked marker in mixed proportions. This anticipates the heading-family pairings noted in `wells1998_notes.md`.

## Enclosures (pp. 153–156, Tables 4.9–4.10, Figure 4.10)

- "The elements described in Chapter 3 as enclosures … can affect the syntactic values of the signs they modify" (p. 153). 275 enclosed sign occurrences: brackets 93, strokes 138, cages 44.
- **Table 4.10:** sign 220 (fish) is mostly medial (239 of 443) and rarely terminal (8); **sign 226 (fish with corner strokes) is "overwhelmingly terminal"** (18 of 36) and "behaves as a terminal marker in some cases" (p. 154); **241: 9 of 10 terminal** (p. 155). Enclosures are used far more at Mohenjo-daro (41) than Harappa (7).
- Figure 4.10: enclosure frequency by phase at Mohenjo-daro DK-G, inversely related to mean text length by phase.
